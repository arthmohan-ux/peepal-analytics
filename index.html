This part is your end-to-end operating guide. It walks through a single, connected journey: understanding what business development actually is at Peepal, learning the services you represent, researching a company and the person inside it, running a first call and then a meeting, using data to lead client conversations, working with delivery, nurturing relationships, and handling the hard moments — difficult reviews, at-risk accounts and dormant accounts. Read it once end-to-end in your first week, then return to individual chapters as situations arise.

This is Peepal's own BD operating system — how we actually win and keep clients. It draws on real Peepal engagements and real prospect conversations. Some figures are approximate and are here to convey scale rather than exact precision.

1. What Business Development Really Means at Peepal

Before any tactics, get the mindset right, because most of what goes wrong in BD comes from a wrong mental model of the job. Peepal sells talent acquisition services — but BD is not the same as recruiting, and it is not the same as chasing leads.

What BD is NOT

If your day looks like the list below, you are doing activity, not business development:

  • Making cold calls and reading a script.
  • Emailing a company profile and waiting.
  • Asking "do you have any requirements?" and hanging up when the answer is no.
  • Listing every Peepal service (RPO, permanent, contract, executive search) in one breath.
  • Following up for the sake of following up — "just checking in" with no reason to exist in the person's inbox.
  • Pushing for vendor empanelment on the first touch.
  • Giving the same pitch to every prospect regardless of who they are or what they do.
Warning Sign

If your first move in a meeting is to pitch, the meeting is usually already lost. The moment you start pitching — before you understand the client — you have almost certainly pitched the wrong thing to the wrong person.

What BD actually is

BD at Peepal is the work of turning a stranger into a trusting client, and a one-off requirement into a long-term account. Concretely, that means:

  • Understanding the company's business — its model, its industry, where it is growing, and how it hires — well enough to have a hypothesis before you speak.
  • Understanding the stakeholder — you are reaching out to a person, not a logo. Their role, tenure, background and incentives shape everything.
  • Identifying current and future hiring needs, including plans the client has not announced yet.
  • Discovering problems the client has not fully articulated — the manager who does not know what they are really solving for, the offer-to-join leak they have not diagnosed.
  • Connecting a business problem to the right Peepal service rather than selling a fixed product.
  • Bringing market intelligence — compensation data, competitor behaviour, candidate-market signals — that the client values even when there is no deal.
  • Representing delivery credibly — knowing enough about how Peepal delivers to speak with conviction and to bring the right delivery colleague in at the right moment.
  • Building long-term relationships and creating trust through follow-through.
  • Finding account-expansion opportunities and making delivery success visible to the client so the account keeps growing.
BD Principle

This is a relationship business. Your job is to be in the client's memory the moment a need arises — "If you are not in my memory when I have the need, and you're not in my WhatsApp, email or LinkedIn, where do I find you?" You earn that place by being useful, not by being persistent.

2. The BD Operating Model: The Lifecycle of an Account

The rest of this playbook follows the natural life of an account, from the first piece of research to a mature, expanding relationship — and, when things go wrong, to recovery or reactivation. Use this map to locate wherever you are with a given prospect.

StageWhat you are doingPlaybook chapter
1. Target & ResearchPick the right company and the right person; build a hypothesis about their likely need.Ch 3–5
2. First ContactCold call or first touch: break the ice, read intent, earn a next step.Ch 6
3. DiscoveryUnderstand current and future needs, internal setup, existing partners, pain points.Ch 7
4. Meeting & PositioningRun a structured meeting; match a service and a proof story; agree next steps.Ch 8–9
5. Data & DeliveryOnce live (or in review), lead with data and partner tightly with delivery.Ch 10–11
6. Nurture & ExpandStay relevant, keep commitments, grow share of the account.Ch 12–13
7. Recover / ReactivateHandle high-stakes reviews, at-risk accounts and dormant accounts.Ch 14–16

Two truths cut across every stage and are worth memorising now, because the whole team returns to them again and again:

  • Data is critical — it cannot be over-emphasised. No story lands with a client unless it arrives in a consumable, data-backed shape. Activity without data is noise.
  • Agility and strategy are among the biggest reasons clients stay — though not the only ones. Being agile (moving fast when priorities shift) and being strategic (telling the client the honest, well-reasoned truth even when it is inconvenient) sit alongside reliable delivery, trust and results in keeping accounts with us for the long term.

3. Know What You Are Selling: Peepal's Services

You cannot diagnose a need if you do not understand the tools available. Peepal delivers talent acquisition through four service lines, all governed by the PEEPAL Way (Part Two covers the philosophy in depth). Learn the plain-English version of each and, crucially, when each fits.

A quick primer on the industry terms

TermWhat it means in plain English
Talent Acquisition (TA)The client's own internal hiring/recruitment team.
RPO (Recruitment Process Outsourcing)Peepal runs part or all of the client's recruitment as an extension of their team — planning, sourcing, interviewing, offers, onboarding.
Permanent hiringPlacing full-time employees on the client's payroll (also called "perm").
Contract / contract staffingPlacing people who work for a fixed period, often on Peepal's or a partner's payroll; fast to scale, lower cost.
C2H (Contract-to-Hire)A contract role that can convert to permanent later.
Executive searchConfidential, headhunted hiring for senior leaders.
GCC (Global Capability Centre)A company's India (or offshore) captive centre doing work for the global parent.
Recruitment funnelThe stages a candidate passes: sourced → screened → interviewed → offered → joined.
Offer-to-join conversionThe share of offered candidates who actually join. A key health metric.

The four service lines

RPO — the flagship

RPO is one broad service line, not a fixed product. Peepal takes end-to-end ownership of the client's recruitment cycle, working as a plug-in extension of their in-house team, and we shape the engagement around what the client needs. Examples of what we can provide under an RPO solution include:

  • Project-Based RPO — a defined hiring project (a specific set of roles, a fixed window).
  • Recruiter on Demand — our recruiters work from the client's own location at no extra cost to the client; Peepal absorbs that cost. This usually lands very well with clients.

These are illustrations of how RPO can be delivered, not separate services to sell. RPO is the model behind the deepest Peepal stories — EY and Telstra — and typically fits clients hiring at volume, building a new team or GCC, or struggling with niche roles.

Permanent hiring

Domain-focused recruiters place full-time hires. This is the natural entry point when a client "already has vendors" — you can start as one more perm vendor and earn your way into a larger RPO relationship by out-performing.

Contract staffing

Built for speed and cost control — Peepal positions contract talent quickly (qualified talent within 24 hours). This is often the first opening in cost-sensitive situations: new GCCs whose first agenda is reducing cost, or product/digital teams inside non-tech businesses that cannot get permanent-headcount budgets (see the Hero MotoCorp and Landmark Group examples in Chapter 5). Contract staffing itself covers several models:

  • Contract-to-Hire (C2H) — a fixed-term role that can convert to permanent later.
  • Contract-to-Contract (C2C) — contract talent engaged through a contracting arrangement.
  • Payroll Management — the client's own employees are placed on Peepal's payroll, with Peepal managing payroll and compliance.

Executive search

Confidential search for senior leaders, focused on leaders who fit the profile and align with the business — not just headhunting. Note the practical lesson from Chapter 9: leadership and managerial hiring is the hardest tier because the client often cannot articulate the real problem.

Service-fit framework — a first hypothesis, not a rule

Enter every conversation with a hypothesis about which service is most likely to fit, then let discovery confirm or correct it.

Do Not Repeat This Mistake

Never present a prospecting conversation as if it were a completed engagement. Hero MotoCorp, Landmark Group and AtoB are prospect discussions — useful to explain how to read a business — not case studies of delivered work. Confusing the two destroys credibility.

4. Research and Preparation

Your meeting almost never happens the moment you reach out — it is usually 72 hours later. That gap is a gift: it is your time to do homework so you walk in sounding like someone worth the client's time. Research has two halves — the company and the person — and the person matters more.

Company research

You do not need exhaustive research; you need enough to form a hypothesis about where the pain is. Look for:

  • What the company does, its business model and industry.
  • India footprint — is there a GCC or capability centre? Which locations?
  • Growth plans, new projects, new business units, recent announcements.
  • Current hiring activity and open roles (LinkedIn Jobs, careers page).
  • Which functions are being built, and which roles are likely to be difficult.
  • Internal TA structure and existing partners.
  • Cost or headcount pressure; whether work is moving into India; whether they are building a team from scratch.
What This Means in Practice

A little structural knowledge lets you pitch precisely. Example: a company with a fixed four-business automotive core (Hero MotoCorp) is unlikely to fund large permanent digital headcount — so contract staffing is the realistic opening. You reasoned your way there from the business model, not from asking. Always write company names in full (e.g. Hero MotoCorp, Landmark Group) so the reference is clear.

Stakeholder research — you are reaching out to a person

The single most important habit: research the person more than the company page. The company page gives you a gist; the person is who decides whether to spend time with you.

  • Their current role and actual responsibilities (which may differ from the title).
  • Geography and business unit they handle; their tenure.
  • Previous organisations — and especially any recruitment-firm or RPO exposure.
  • Their LinkedIn posts, comments and reposts; events they attended.
  • Mutual connections and any prior Peepal interaction (check CRM history).
  • Likely influence and decision-making authority; can they refer Peepal internally?
BD Principle

One of the strongest patterns we see: people who have themselves worked in a recruitment firm understand the pain of hiring far better and are much easier to connect with than someone who has only ever worked corporate. Screen for this in a person's background — it changes how you pitch.

Where to gather it

Use LinkedIn (profile, posts, the employee list filtered by location to see where teams sit), CRM history, internal relationships, previous client history, delivery-team knowledge, relevant Peepal case studies, and general hiring/industry intelligence. A neat LinkedIn trick: filter employees by city to infer team structure — "a lot of your tech team is in Hyderabad while you're hiring analytics in Bangalore." Even if you are slightly wrong, the person corrects you, and now they are engaged.

Check for an existing connection first

Before you reach out, check whether Peepal already has a link to the person or the company — it changes your whole opening. Specifically, look for:

  • Whether the person is a member of the TA Dialogues group.
  • Whether we have worked with this company before, or currently work with them.
  • Whether anyone at the person's current or previous company is already known to us.

All of this is checkable through the CRM — previous clients and current clients — and the TA Dialogues group. A genuine existing connection is the strongest and most natural way in.

Ethical name-dropping

"If you're not name-dropping, you're not doing sales." Connecting a person to someone from their past organisation or your network builds instant rapport. But there are firm limits:

  • Do reference genuine connections: a shared former employer, a delivery colleague who worked their vertical, a mutual contact, a TA Dialogues event they attended.
  • Do not fabricate familiarity. Name-dropping people you have no real link to, on a first cold call, reads as creepy and burns trust. As the team noted, it lands far better on the second call, once a thread exists.
Warning Sign

Congratulating someone on a new role can help them feel seen — but overdone or ill-judged flattery ("I saw you're running the whole thing") can misfire, especially if you have the facts slightly wrong. Acknowledge; do not fawn. If you get their scope wrong, that is sometimes useful — they will correct you and engage — but never invent a relationship.

5. Knowing What to Sell: Enter With a Hypothesis

A weak BD person walks in with a pitch. A strong one walks in with a hypothesis about the client's problem and a small number of questions to test it. The direction of your services should be inferred from how the business operates — then confirmed by questions.

Reading the business to infer the service

Note

The company examples below are only illustrations, based on earlier situations where we worked with or pitched to similar companies. Do not assume they describe the current situation at the company you are approaching — a business can change, and your prospect may be nothing like the example. They are here to show you how to think, not what is true today. Always confirm with your own research and questions.

Hero MotoCorp — budgets dictate contract

Basic research shows Hero MotoCorp is built around four automotive businesses. Any digital ambition is small and has to fight for budget; without budget, permanent headcount will not open up. So the realistic entry is contract staffing, and the person to talk to is the vertical head (the digital & architecture lead), who has visibility of whether the business actually exists — not TA. For contract he has a mandate to use preferred vendors with sorted pricing; the counter — "If we match that pricing, do we get an opportunity?" — keeps the door open and confirms he is the decision-maker.

Landmark Group — a cost-first GCC

Landmark Group's India GCC is retail-centred and building its digital presence, with early work moving from the Middle East to India. A new GCC's first agenda is usually cost reduction — so it will often start with contract staffing, and often with preferred partners. To win, you either match on price or go directly to the business person, because TA will just say "we have 100 partners."

AtoB — find who actually gives business

At AtoB, the pattern we have seen is that senior directors are the right entry point; VPs will not give business. You discover this by working the top-down approach and building a pattern across many conversations. In a space where you have no specialty, expect roughly 30 conversations to yield 1 real opportunity — but those 30 teach you which titles convert.

What This Means in Practice

In a physical meeting you can read where a person's likeliness is going and steer. On a call you cannot — so your questions are the only way to find the direction of services. Ask early whether it is a lean deal, whether they have tried RPO before, whether they burned their hands with it. If RPO will clearly be shot down, pivot to a regular vendor relationship you can later convert.

The "toughest role" entry technique

A powerful first-cold-call move: lead with the hardest role in their space, not the easy one. Roles already posted on LinkedIn usually have an internal pipeline; the roles that are genuinely difficult (cyber inside a clinical-intelligence firm; quant, risk, modelling inside banking) are where the client struggles. Pitching the tough area does two things: it is where you can actually win entry, and even a wrong guess extracts the truth.

Warning Sign

This technique can work well, but use it carefully. Research the company properly first and make sure the tough role you lead with is genuinely relevant to them — a mismatched or random guess wastes the opening and can make you look unprepared. Lead with a real reach-area for that specific business, not a generic "hard role."

Sample Language

"I'm sure you're building a cyber team — cyber is a reach area, and these are the organisations we've done it for." If they say "no, we're hiring data science," you reply: "Oh, I thought you were building cyber — on data science, here's what we've done." Either way you now know their real space and have a proof point ready. (Use with judgement: some stakeholders take "your internal team couldn't close this" personally. Read the person.)

Note

Distinguish clearly, in your own head and notes, between: a prospecting example (a live conversation), a completed engagement (delivered work you can cite as a case study), and a hypothetical recommendation (what you think would fit). Never blur them when talking to a client.

6. The First Call

The first call has one job: earn the right to a next conversation. It is not where you close, and it is emphatically not where you pitch everything you offer.

Before you dial

  • Do your stakeholder homework (Chapter 4) and decide your icebreaker in advance.
  • Know one or two relevant proof points for their industry/space — have the client dashboard open so you never fumble names (see Chapter 17).
  • Pick the hardest, most relevant role area as your opening hypothesis.
  • Do not over-research to the point of paralysis. A cold call may last 30–60 seconds; a leader's time is scarce. Do smart, reusable research — and if you invest deeply in one company, write it up so you can reuse it for similar ones.

Open with an icebreaker, not a pitch

Always start by breaking the ice — however uncomfortable that feels. Good icebreakers: something they posted on LinkedIn, a mutual connection, an event ("You attended the SHRM unconference — how was it?"), or your own TA Dialogues. The icebreaker then flows into "what exactly are you handling? Which territories are you leading?" You want the client talking first. If you make the first sales pitch, you have almost certainly pitched the wrong thing to the wrong person.

BD Principle

"If they are not able to give you five or ten minutes, you're not being a good BD person. A smart BD person pulls the person out of a meeting with their pitch." Nobody owes you their time — it is your job to make the conversation interesting enough that they find it. "I have five minutes" is a test, not a limit.

Reading intent

Different people arrive with different intent, and your approach must flex. Some are closed on the phone but will book a meeting; some take four calls before a meeting; some are simply confused about what they need. Your aim on the call is to break the interaction down enough that they reveal why they are even spending time with you — that is more valuable than any answer you give.

Handling the standard objections

They say…Do NOT…Instead
"I only have five minutes / can we do this quickly?"Rush into a feature dump.Treat it as a test. Lead with the single most relevant, interesting thing. If you're interesting, they find the time.
"Send me an email."Just send it and disappear.Agree — then immediately clock the next step: "I'll send it. When can I speak to you again? How does next Tuesday look?" Never leave without a next touch booked.
"No current requirement."Hang up / write them off.Shift to the future: 3/6/9/12-month plans, growth, projects being explored. Offer value (reports, insights) and set a relevant follow-up.
"We already have vendors / partners."Concede and leave.That is normal — "even at zero dependency you still have periodic needs." Position to show what you can bring that the current setup does not: speed, quality, or simply more market reach.
"We have a strong internal team, no gaps."Accept "no gaps" at face value.Even 100%-internal teams experiment with a vendor to learn what they're missing — speed, quality, or market visibility. Offer a small, low-risk way to demonstrate that.
"Is this about vendor empanelment?"Say yes and get filed away.Reframe: "Not primarily — eventually maybe, but today I just want to understand how you're planning your hiring for this quarter and next year." (This exact reframe converted a Citibank stakeholder from a brush-off to a meeting.)
"Give me some time / let me look at my day."Leave it open-ended.Propose a concrete next contact: "Let me call you at 4pm Monday" or "Can I WhatsApp you tomorrow?" Not every call converts — but if most end here, either you're reaching the wrong person or you don't sound right on calls.

The one non-negotiable: secure the next step

If you do not clock your next call or meeting before hanging up, you will lose the lead. Even a "send me an email" must be followed by "…and when can we speak again about it?" Your agenda on a first call is never "pitch" and never merely "follow up" — it is to explore a problem and come back with a solution or an insight, and to book the next contact.

Questions to Ask

Which territories / business units do you lead right now?

How is your hiring set up internally — team size, and do you use partners?

What does the next 3, 6, 9, 12 months look like for headcount?

Are there projects or expansions you're exploring where we could help?

Have you used RPO before — how did that go?

Worked call examples

Real calls that show these moves in action. Like the diagnosis examples in Chapter 5, these are illustrations of technique — not a script, and not the situation at any company you are calling now.

ANZ — the value-led "no requirement" save

A stakeholder had no hiring scope that quarter. Rather than push, the BD person offered to keep sharing useful reports and skill studies and to plan the next quarter together — positioning Peepal as a complement, not a replacement. It earned a "keep sending, sounds good," and kept the relationship warm for when a need appears.

PayPal — repeat back to surface the truth

A stakeholder first claimed "no partners." Repeating the statement back — "So you're saying you don't work with any recruitment partners at all?" — prompted them to clarify: contract via partners, permanent mostly internal with a small team leaning on referrals. That intelligence then becomes reusable, ethical name-drop context with a comparable payments company.

Citibank — the empanelment reframe

The turning-point question was "Is this about vendor empanelment?" The save: "Not primarily — eventually maybe; today I just want to understand your hiring plans for this quarter and next year, and you're the right person." That converted a brush-off into a booked meeting.

Blue Yonder — why a "good" meeting can still ghost

A pleasant meeting still went nowhere because the BD person left without the core answers — no visibility of requirements, no read on future growth, an unclear partner/internal-team picture, and only a vague "weekly follow-up" plan. The lesson: a meeting that fills none of the discovery boxes is a failed meeting, however friendly it felt. That is why prospects ghost.

7. Discovery and Need Analysis

Discovery is where BD is won. If you leave a relationship meeting without answers to a core set of questions, the prospect will "ghost" you — not out of rudeness, but because you gave them no reason to remember you. Build the habit of leaving every conversation with these boxes filled, or knowing exactly which are still question marks.

The discovery checklist

You do not need all of this on call one, but you need a plan to get it. Treat every unanswered item as a reason for the next conversation.

  • Requirements: current open roles, and the roles that stay open longest.
  • Future: 3/6/9/12-month hiring plans; growth and expansion; new BUs or locations.
  • Internal capacity: size and strength of the internal TA team.
  • Existing partners: how many vendors, and how are they performing?
  • RPO history: have they tried RPO? Did they burn their hands?
  • Pain points: difficult roles, long-open roles, hiring locations, interview bottlenecks, feedback delays, compensation issues, offer-to-join conversion, employer-brand challenges.
  • Business-unit specifics: is one BU producing most of the pain or dropouts?
  • Process & decision-making: approval stages, who the decision-makers are, procurement, and — critically — what "success" looks like to them.
BD Principle

Frame the meeting honestly: "My meeting is a relationship meeting." From it you want three things — a follow-up meeting agreed, clarity on immediate vs future requirements, and an understanding of their partners and internal team. Ask these bluntly but warmly, "candidly and bluntly," because without the answers you go back assuming — and assumption is the biggest problem in BD.

Ask conversationally, not as an interrogation

The list above is your internal checklist — not a script you fire at the client. Weave the questions through a genuine conversation that starts from the icebreaker. A useful softening technique when someone is evasive or says something you doubt: repeat their sentence back to them. "So you're saying ABC as an organisation doesn't work with any recruitment vendors at all — is that right?" People instinctively start defending or clarifying, and you get the real answer (e.g., "well, for contract we do have partners, but perm is mostly internal"). It is also a clean way to handle someone putting you down: repeat it back, and they walk it back.

8. Meeting Preparation

There are two kinds of important meetings — a new-prospect meeting and an existing-client review — and both fail the same way: walking in blind. Entering a review "blindfolded," not knowing it will be about your numbers, is how good accounts are put at risk. Never do it.

Preparing for a new-prospect meeting

Before the meeting, the whole attending team should be able to answer:

  • Who is attending, and what is each attendee's likely agenda?
  • What is Peepal's objective, and what outcome do we want?
  • The client's business context, current hiring situation and likely pain points.
  • Which service options and which proof points / case studies are relevant.
  • Which roles Peepal has previously closed in this space (have the dashboard ready).
  • Possible objections, and internal delivery feasibility.
  • Who from Peepal should attend, who leads which part, and why.
What This Means in Practice

Investing your research the day before is fine — you do not need to prepare a week out. The night before a meeting, do your deep research, talk to the delivery person, and arrive with an initial plan and approach that you can adapt live. One BD person books the meeting a week or two out precisely so there is time to prepare properly.

Preparing for an existing-client review

A review is a data meeting. Come having already interrogated the numbers so you know the story before the client tells it to you. Prepare the full funnel and the likely flashpoints:

  • Requirements, submissions, interviews, offers, joinees.
  • Offer-to-join conversion and where the drops are concentrated.
  • Notice periods, CTC-related losses, competitor offers, candidate withdrawals.
  • Client-side delays (feedback, approvals) and business-unit differences.
  • Delivery bottlenecks and the corrective actions you will propose.
BD Principle

Never enter an important review without understanding the data and the likely concerns. The data usually reaches you only 12–18 hours before the meeting, so you cannot fix it then — the fix is an ongoing data-collection discipline (Chapter 10). "Even sitting on that data is important" — reading it in advance is what lets you lead the room instead of being cornered by it.

TOOLKIT

Meeting-Preparation Checklist

  • Attendees (both sides) and each person's likely agenda.
  • Peepal objective + desired outcome.
  • Client context, current hiring, likely pain points.
  • Service options + matched case study + roles previously closed (dashboard).
  • Likely objections + delivery feasibility.
  • Who from Peepal attends, who leads which part, why (bring delivery if they add real depth).

9. Running the Meeting

A strong client meeting moves through a natural arc. You will not always hit every step, but this is the shape to aim for.

  1. Relevant opening / icebreaker — grounded in their role or recent activity.
  2. Understand the stakeholder — what they own, what they care about.
  3. Discovery — the checklist from Chapter 7, conversationally.
  4. Clarify the problem — play it back; make sure you and they agree what it is.
  5. Test assumptions — challenge gently where the stated problem may not be the real one.
  6. Summarise the need — in their words.
  7. Present the relevant capability — only now, and only what fits.
  8. Use a matched case study — one story that mirrors their situation (Part Two).
  9. Agree next steps — concrete, dated, owned.

The balances you must hold

BalanceHow to hold it
Listening vs presentingDefault to listening; present only after the problem is clear.
Relationship vs business outcomeBe a friend and get answers — good talk with no business is a failure mode too.
Confidence vs humilityConfident about capability; humble about what you don't yet know.
Correcting the client vs preserving faceReframe with data, never embarrass — see the "four of 80" example in Chapter 10.
Challenging vs trustChallenge assumptions as a partner would, always in the client's interest.
Meeting politicsRead who needs to look good; help them, because you must keep working with them.
What This Means in Practice

Politics are real. In one review the stakeholder's own boss was in the room, so she needed to look in control of the RPO. The Peepal lead let her save face — "we'll get it to zero" — while still, gently, correcting the exaggeration with data. You have to keep working with her afterwards, so you rub nobody's nose in it.

The hardest tier: managerial and leadership hiring

Expect leadership and — especially — managerial roles to be the trickiest, because the client often does not know what they are actually solving for. The value you add is asking the questions that put the role in a box instead of a black box.

  • On briefing calls, ask them to put a percentage on manager vs hands-on. "50/50" is what everyone wants and nobody gets — so ask for the compromise scenario. That single question surfaces the real priority (e.g., a 70% manager / 30% technical need because there is nobody to guide the team).
  • Diagnose why the manager is needed: to manage stakeholders, to make the team stronger, to oversee deep technical work, or to shake up a settled team? Each points to a different hire.
  • Watch for scale mismatch: someone who has handled a 70-person team is a real risk for a role that must scale a 100-person team to 200 — "the dynamics of handling 100 are very different from 7." A known black box is still hard, but far better than an unknown one.
Questions to Ask

If 50/50 manager-vs-hands-on isn't achievable, what's your compromise — and why?

Is this hire to manage stakeholders, strengthen the team, oversee technical depth, or change how a settled team works?

What team size must this person scale to, and by when?

Is your current team so senior/settled that the new manager must be strong enough to reshape it?

10. Using Data Strategically

Data is your single most important tool in a client conversation — it cannot be over-emphasised. But data merely displayed is useless, even harmful. The skill is to interpret it, diagnose from it, and build a client narrative with it. Move deliberately along this ladder:

RungWhat it looks like
1. Report activityX submissions, Y interviews, Z offers. (Necessary, not persuasive.)
2. Identify patternsOne BU is producing most drops; losses cluster at 45–60 day notice.
3. DiagnoseWhy? CTC mismatch, competitor counter-offers, portal friction, one panellist.
4. Recommend actionRe-open borderline CTC cases; add a co-panellist; create a candidate video.
5. Build a narrativePosition Peepal as the strategic partner solving the offer-to-join problem.

Worked examples the team uses

Note

The examples below are real situations from specific accounts, included to show the technique. The exact numbers (e.g. "four of 80") belong to those particular reviews — they are illustrations of how to read and reframe data, not benchmarks or rules that apply to every client. Always run your own account's actual numbers.

  • Offer-to-join conversion: a healthy benchmark is roughly 70%. If you're at 66%, don't shrug ("that's the market"). Either push 66 toward 70, or categorise the drops so you can say precisely why you're losing people — and act on it.
  • CTC-driven drops: if a large chunk of losses are CTC, surface it, and identify the borderline candidates who are still convertible: "Would you still be interested if we re-engaged these?" That changes the whole narrative from excuse to strategy.
  • Notice-period losses (a specific real case): in one review a client felt "many" candidates were being lost to long notice periods. The tracker for that account told a different story — of 80 selected candidates, only four were 45–60-day cases. This is what happened in that instance; treat it as an example of checking the claim against the data, not as a typical ratio.
  • One business unit dropping disproportionately: flag it and investigate — it may signal a specific competitor targeting those candidates, or an internal issue in that BU.
  • Competitor counter-offers: delivery hears this first. Example: Deloitte fast-tracks anyone holding a Big-4 (EY/KPMG GDS) offer — sometimes one round, an offer within a day. You may not be able to stop it, but making it visible ("this is where your offers are leaking, and here's how they're doing it") is itself strategic value.

Correcting an exaggerated perception — respectfully

When a client over-states a problem, do not accept it and do not bluntly contradict it. Interrogate the data live and reframe:

  1. Confirm the data is trusted: "Is the tracker we're both looking at accurate?"
  2. Apply the filter in front of them: of 80 candidates, four are notice-period drops.
  3. Concede the ideal ("it should be zero, agreed") and give delivery some cushion.
  4. Commit to a fix: go back to the recruiter, find why those cases were processed, put a process in place.
  5. Land it gently, mindful of politics: "It's four out of 80 — we'll get it to zero," so the stakeholder keeps face in front of their boss.
Do Not Repeat This Mistake

Do not walk into a review relying on memory or on data you haven't read. "If you're relying on memory, it's not going to help." Track meetings and numbers in a system, set clear timelines for when a lead goes cold, and read the funnel before every review. Blindfolded reviews are how good accounts are lost.

Client-Review Data Checklist

  • Notice-period losses; CTC-related losses (with the borderline-convertible list).
  • Competitor offers/counter-offers; candidate withdrawals.
  • Client-side delays (feedback/approvals); business-unit differences.
  • Delivery bottlenecks + the corrective action for each.

11. Working With Delivery

Delivery is your closest partner and your richest source of truth. They have the candidate touch-points, so they know things you cannot: why candidates drop, what competitors are offering, how a role really behaves in the market. Bring them in deliberately.

Partner with delivery for:

  • Role understanding, market and compensation intelligence, candidate behaviour, competitor activity.
  • Relevant proof points, proposal prep, meeting prep, feasibility checks.
  • Client reviews, escalations, account expansion and creating new case studies.

When should a delivery person be in the client meeting?

Bring delivery when they can add value you cannot credibly fake — deep role/skill discussion, competency comparisons, market specifics. In several first meetings, delivery presence directly moved the deal: a strong delivery-led conversation led a client to ask for C2H and full-time commercials; in another, a delivery colleague who genuinely knew the domain (her spouse worked in the space) added real credibility.

What This Means in Practice

Know your own limits. If a delivery colleague would add insight on similar-organisation roles or competencies, do not attempt to fake that depth yourself — "you will do a subdued job." Route the technical portion to delivery and own the relationship and narrative.

Note

When re-engaging a client, bringing a new delivery face is mainly worth doing when the previous engagement went badly and the old association could get in the way. It is not always necessary — if the account simply went quiet, the existing delivery relationship may be an asset. Read the situation before deciding.

Market and competitive intelligence from delivery

Because delivery talks to candidates constantly, they hear the market before anyone else. This intelligence is something you can give away to stay relevant to a client and to sharpen their assumptions with evidence — sourcing it is a reason to talk to delivery often. Useful examples:

  • Competitor counter-offers: Deloitte fast-tracks candidates holding a Big-4 (EY/KPMG GDS) offer — sometimes a single round and an offer within a day. You may not be able to stop it, but making it visible ("this is where your offers are leaking, and here is how") is itself strategic value.
  • Notice-period reality: clients demanding immediate or 15-day joiners must accept higher drops and be willing to counter-offer (often 20%+ over current). Cover this ground before committing.
  • Talent circulation: in tight pools (quant, advanced analytics, and increasingly AI), the same small set of people circulates among a few financial/insurance/risk firms — keeping candidates "warm" makes sense only in these niches, not generally.
  • Managerial attrition is characteristic-driven: managers rarely leave for money alone — one joiner quit in days for lack of a challenging project; another left purely for "a better manager." Diagnose the reason, do not assume CTC.

12. Follow-up, Nurture and Staying Relevant

This is a relationship business, so you never really "step back." But there is a world of difference between chasing and nurturing. Chasing is being "that company that just keeps calling" until they block you. Nurturing is staying in memory by being useful.

This……not this
Staying relevant — every touch has valueChasing — "just following up" with no reason
Sharing a report they'll actually useAsking again if they have requirements
A purposeful reason to appearBeing continuously "salesy" until they tune out

Value-led touchpoints

Every follow-up should carry a reason to exist in the person's inbox:

  • TA Dialogues invitations (Peepal's own events — two of the largest accounts came through them).
  • Relevant market/skill reports, hiring intelligence, compensation insights, candidate-market observations.
  • A relevant client example or a business announcement about them; a genuine congratulations.
  • A relevant introduction; a quarterly-planning conversation.
BD Principle

"Following up" is never a legitimate agenda for a meeting or email. The agenda is to explore, add value, and stay relevant. Position yourself as a complement to their team, not a replacement: "We're not here to replace your team — we're here to complement it." That is where Peepal's 20 years of experience earns its place.

Cadence — relevance, not a fixed clock

Do not run one identical cadence for every prospect. Let cadence flex on: opportunity timing, the client's hiring plans, relationship strength, any explicit indication they gave, stakeholder seniority, the relevance of what you're sharing, and whether an action is pending. One BD approach that works: for passive/dormant accounts, a weekly value touch rather than monthly — because busy leaders get 5–6 emails a day, and weekly (done tastefully, not daily) keeps you visible without becoming the nuisance. Others will merit fortnightly or occasional. The test is always: does this touch give them something?

13. Client Commitments and Reliability

Reliability is not perfection; it is showing up consistently, especially when things are hard. It is built — or destroyed — in small commitments.

  • Do not make commitments casually, and never commit numbers on a call before checking feasibility with delivery. The client will hold you to whatever number you say — and ask why 10 wasn't 25.
  • If you must give a number, commit small and certain: "Let me collaborate with delivery; you'll have my answer within 24 hours." Then a low, safe number the team will beat — under-promise, over-deliver.
  • "24 hours is 24 hours — not 25." If you promised a response window, hit it. Beating it is great; missing it costs conviction.
  • Repeatedly missing small commitments quietly destroys trust. Predictability is a series of kept small promises.
Peepal Way in Practice

This is the PEEPAL Way principle of Predictable in daily behaviour: Achievability (be upfront about what's doable across time, quality and price), Visibility (keep them informed), and Readiness (flag what's needed before it becomes a problem). And Reliable: consistency in communication, execution and ownership — a "spotless record" is built one 24-hour promise at a time.

14. Handling High-Stakes Reviews and At-Risk Accounts

Not every meeting is friendly. A tense review or an account that is drifting is survivable — and can even deepen trust — if you handle it with preparation and ownership rather than excuses.

The recovery sequence

  1. Prepare — never go in blind. A difficult meeting is manageable when you see it coming and have the numbers. "Even if it is a bad story, that story should be visible to you in advance."
  2. Understand the client's point of view first. Read the pressure they are under and see the situation from their chair before you respond.
  3. Own the conversation, don't deflect. Acknowledge directly and without defensiveness what needs to improve — ownership is what earns goodwill.
  4. Lead with the numbers and the honest root causes, then move straight to the fix.
  5. Validate the plan internally before you commit. Never commit a number on the call. "Whatever is your word — even 24 hours — 24 hours is 24 hours." Come back with a delivery-checked commitment.
  6. Make a realistic, small commitment and then beat it. Under-promise, over-deliver, and let the result rebuild confidence.
  7. Monitor and rebuild. After the meeting, run the internal rigor: what is the number, and what work guarantees it? Rebuild confidence through delivery, not words.
BD Principle

Offer conviction, not spin. Personal ownership — "give us this window, and we will fix it" — is what earns a second chance. Handled this way, a hard engagement demonstrates ownership, transparency, resilience, adaptation and accountability.

16. Reactivating a Dormant Account

Accounts go quiet for two very different reasons, and the right approach depends on which one you are dealing with. Work out which scenario you are in before you plan the conversation.

Scenario 1 — Dormant simply because there were no open roles

Nothing went wrong; the relationship just went quiet because there was no hiring. Here the job is straightforward re-engagement:

  1. Focus on securing a meeting and re-opening the conversation warmly.
  2. Understand their current hiring needs — what has changed since you last spoke, and what the next few months look like.
  3. Identify the relevant roles you can genuinely help with now, and line up a proof point that matches.

There is no history to overcome — so lean on the existing relationship (and your existing delivery team) rather than resetting anything.

Scenario 2 — The previous engagement went sour

The account went cold because a past engagement disappointed. Here the job is a genuine fresh start, not a defence of what happened:

  1. Lead with a fresh start, not a defence. "I am here with a new team and a clear intent to make this work." There are at least five versions of any old story; re-litigating it is a wormhole.
  2. Ask what the client needs now and align on what success looks like.
  3. Bring a new, accountable team — a new delivery face so the client sees genuine change.
  4. Propose a small pilot — a couple of weeks to a month — with measurable success criteria agreed up front.
  5. Review after the defined period. Rebuild trust through fresh evidence, not old promises.
What This Means in Practice

In Scenario 2, if the client raises the past, take light accountability and pivot forward: "here is what has since changed, and I will assign you a new team" — then move to "what did success look like for you, and let us channel our energy there." Keep the conversation about the future you can deliver, not the history you cannot change.

TOOLKIT

Account-Reactivation Plan (dormant / soured)

  1. Open fresh — do not defend the past you weren't part of.
  2. Ask what specifically failed; listen.
  3. Bring a new, accountable delivery face (not the old one).
  4. Propose a small pilot (2 weeks–1 month) with agreed success metrics.
  5. Set a review date; rebuild trust through evidence.
  6. If they raise history: light accountability + "what did success look like for you?"

17. Personal Development

BD is a craft you improve by deliberate practice, and most of the improvement is unglamorous self-review. The role demands it.

  • Record your calls and listen back. You may be great in person and sound "rubbish" on a call — you will not know until you hear it. Catch your filler words ("like," "generally"). It is cringe to listen to yourself; do it anyway.
  • Review your video-call body language. Gestures and presence matter on video; nobody will teach you this — watch yourself.
  • Practise case-study narration until you never fumble. In multi-person pitches, BD people freeze when asked for client examples — practise the deepest stories (EY, Telstra) cold.
  • Keep proof points at your fingertips. Use the internal client dashboard (industry in, roles-closed out) live during meetings so you can recall the right example instantly.
  • Learn client names, roles and sectors. Sales runs on names; a weak memory is a liability you must actively compensate for.
  • Do weekly self-analysis: review call-to-meeting conversion, response patterns, what worked and what did not, and set next week's focus. Reaching the point where a call needs no prep comes only from making many calls and absorbing many disappointments.
BD Principle

Smartness in sales is pattern recognition: the more you do it, the more you can read a person and a business at a glance. Every call, meeting and self-review builds your patterns — which backgrounds convert, which titles give business, which openings make people listen. Do the volume, review honestly, and the patterns come.

Tag: Full case study — the flagship account.

Context & engagement model

EY GDS hires technology talent at scale, pan-India. Peepal engages as an RPO partner, effectively operating as EY's talent-acquisition team for its share of hiring.

Initial situation & the complexity

  • Specialised the delivery team for EY: separate sourcing, interview-management and offer-management teams, plus a dedicated stakeholder SPOC.

  • Peepal is the top partner, closing over 70% of open requirements — so embedded that when a role opens, the business asks for it to go to Peepal because they know it will close quickly.
  • Public (client-safe) figures: 5-year partnership; 900+ exclusive roles closed; 1,500+ offers; 1,300+ joinees, pan-India.

Expertise — separated sourcing/interview/offer teams; owns the whole journey at scale.

CASE 2 — Telstra (Australian Telecom Giant)

CASE 3 — Xylem (Global Water-Technology GCC Setup)

Tag: Full case study — GCC build + employer-branding. The best story for "we're setting up in India and nobody knows our brand." (Xylem: a global water-technology company — "the largest converter of sea water to potable water.")

Context & entry

Xylem had a manufacturing plant in Baroda (under 100 people) and was standing up an India GCC. First contact came via a stakeholder in China, who flew to India specifically to meet Peepal — an intense working session on sourcing numbers, CTC bands, comparable organisations and live sourcing.

The build — Peepal well beyond recruitment

  • The client's India setup took time, so Peepal used the runway to add value: mapped office locations (Manyata, Thanisandra, Bellandur, Electronic City) and connected Xylem to an internal consultant on EPF, Karnataka registration and co-working options.
  • The GCC leadership sat offshore and the offer-approver was in Baroda — so Peepal ran the compensation reality for them.

Interventions

  • Compensation mapping: Baroda vs Bangalore CTC differed roughly 3x. Peepal showcased top Bangalore candidates against Baroda benchmarks and reset approval to a workable ~2x.
  • Sequencing: advised running top-down and bottom-up simultaneously — hire juniors for a baseline while pursuing managers with later joining dates — so the India leadership wasn't left overseeing only juniors.
  • Employer brand: because Xylem was little-known in India, Peepal created explainer material/video shipped with every JD, made LinkedIn posters for hiring managers, and ran a drive to lift Glassdoor visibility.
  • Onboarding & culture (no HR on the ground): Peepal ran onboarding — welcome cards, bouquets — and organised festival events to keep the new team engaged. Our recruiters effectively acted as Xylem's employer-brand and HR voice.

Results

  • Around 100 hires overall; a few skills had 5–10 hires, most were unique. Strong post-join retention once the culture work took hold — helped by the genuine global exposure Xylem gave joiners.
Peepal Way in Practice

Strategic — challenged the 3x comp assumption; sequenced top-down + bottom-up; built the India org, not just filled seats.

Expertise — GCC-setup depth: location, registration, comp mapping, onboarding.

Intelligent — diagnosed that offer conversion hinged on brand, and built brand assets to fix it.

Reliable — did the unglamorous, unasked work (offices, Glassdoor, events) to make the client succeed.

Use This Story When

A company is setting up a GCC / India centre from scratch, especially an unknown-in-India brand.

The pain is employer brand / offer conversion, or multi-location comp mismatch (metro vs non-metro).

The client needs a partner who will go beyond vacancies into org-building, onboarding and retention.

Client-Safe Version

Peepal helped a global water-technology company stand up its India GCC end-to-end — location and setup guidance, compensation mapping across cities, and employer-brand and onboarding interventions that strengthened offer conversion — placing around 100 hires while building the new India organisation.

CASE 4 — Salesforce.com (Senior Engineering Volume + Authenticity)

Tag: Full case study / strong proof point — process innovation. Best for candidate-authenticity and application-friction problems.

Context & problem

A project to hire around 148 senior software engineers. Two problems emerged: heavy Hyderabad concentration with candidate-authenticity/proxy concerns, and a mandatory application portal whose navigation was hard enough that strong candidates were dropping mid-application.

Interventions

  • Authenticity control: introduced Aadhaar (ID) verification within the interview to counter proxy/fudged candidates.
  • Portal video: proactively created an instructional walkthrough video, shipped with every JD, so candidates could navigate the portal — materially improving conversion.
  • Held pace while adding two verification steps — proof that quality controls and speed can coexist.

Results

Portal-stage drop-off fell sharply once the video was in play, and candidate authenticity improved — a small, strategic change that fixed a large funnel leak.

Peepal Way in Practice

Strategic/Intelligent — a tiny change (a portal video) fixed a large funnel leak; that's diagnosis, not brute force.

Expertise — introduced integrity controls (Aadhaar) without sacrificing speed.

Use This Story When

The prospect worries about candidate authenticity / proxies, or has a clunky application process losing candidates.

You want to show Peepal spots and fixes non-obvious funnel leaks.
